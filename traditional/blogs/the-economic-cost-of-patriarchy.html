The Economic Cost of Patriarchy

September 2021

Half of the work in the world is unpaid, a large portion of which falls on women. It is not a shocking statement; it is something we see every day. There is a glaring inequality of division of unpaid work. The effect of inequality came to the forefront during the lockdown. When the world shut down, the workplace came home. Recognizing the burden that fell on the shoulders of the women of the household became easier to see. Discussions around the problematic aspects of the phenomenon started to gain heat.

There will always exist some form of unpaid labor carried out as a part of everyday lives. Unpaid labor includes household chores, care work, especially for elders and children. Oxfam estimates the value of work undertaken by women accounts for $10.9 trillion worldwide. This number is greater than the total money made by the top 50 largest companies in the world.

It is impractical to delegate all such free labor to services that are part of the markets. The existence and undertaking of unpaid chores are not the problems. The problem lies in the skewed division of this labor. India exhibits one of the widest gaps in the distribution of uncompensated work between genders. While Indian women spend 6 hours a day on this work, their male counterparts only spend 52 minutes. The gap is smallest in the Nordic countries. Irrespective of the country, continent, or region, the fact remains that women across the world spent more time engaged in unpaid work than men.

The problem with insufficient compensation paid to women for their work extends beyond the domestic sphere, in the workplace, where men and women deserve to receive equal compensation for their work. Data collected in recent years have shown that women receive only 70% of what men gain for the same service. There is also the problem of the disproportionate presence of women in managerial positions. Women make up almost half of the workforce. Yet, they hold less than 30% of senior management positions.

While women receive only a fraction of their male counterparts, the taxes and prices on goods marketed for them are higher. This phenomenon is called the Pink Tax. The word Pink Tax itself is a testament to the pervasiveness of gender stereotypes in everyday life. We must, however, understand it to gain a fuller picture.

The Pink Tax is a phenomenon where products marketed to women cost way higher than those intended for men for no substantial reason.

Pink Tax is a multilayered problem. Why the term Pink Tax? Pink is a color generally associated with femininity. Many of these products that fall in the higher price bracket have pink packaging. In many parts of the world, these products are taxed higher by governments and priced differently by the sellers and producers. Hence, the term Pink tax.

One of the most important examples of Pink Tax is the Tax imposed on sanitary pads and tampons. In India, there is a 12% luxury tax on Sanitary products. When a no-tax category exists for necessities, it feels right for sanitary pads to be in that category.

This bias is evident in the prices of clothing for men and women. While we can buy a simple men’s shirt for less than 500 rupees, the same isn’t the case for women’s shirts. They are more expensive, and in some countries, women’s clothing is taxed higher than men’s clothing. In most brands, buying a simple t-shirt from the women’s section is a challenge irrespective of its cost. There are at least a dozen combinations of necklines, cloth type, presence of sequins, sleeve types, etc. decision-making becomes a complex probability problem. The result is often the impossibility of finding the right combination of all variables despite paying a higher price.

Another glaringly obvious bias is in toiletries. Female-coded toiletries are often more expensive than male-coded ones. Despite the two offering the same benefits, the pink Tax significantly raises the price of the products marketed to women. Unisex products too are cheaper than products made explicitly for women.

One reason for the continuing presence of Pink Tax is the lack of constitutional safeguards to prevent such differential pricing. While some countries have tried to change policy to remove Pink Tax, it has largely proved unsuccessful.

To reach a society equal in all aspects of life, we need policy reforms and constitutional amendments to ensure them. This requires the presence of women in the decision-making bodies of the government. While the representation of women in public office in India is higher than in most other countries, it is not proportional to the percentage of the population that women make up. Women in India makeup almost 50% of the population but only make up 15% of the Lok Sabha.

The multi-tiered inequalities in treatment of the genders reach further than what meets the eye. The root cause is societal norms that dictate a specific stereotypical idea of work, behavior, and role that people are expected to play as a part of society. The root cause is a patriarchal society that has drilled into our minds that women are the default caregivers. Their work is their duty. To be a part of the economic labor force, political decision-makers, judicial personnel, or law enforcement, women must jump through multiple hurdles to prove their worth.

Iceland ranks first in the Global Gender Gap report, a testament to the most gender-equal society to exist today. This is primarily due to the government reforms. Social safety net programs provide care for children and older people. Iceland has one of the highest rates of women in labor force participation.

This change was made possible by a nationwide strike that Icelandic women undertook in 1975. They refused to cook, clean, or care for children for an entire day. On that day — now remembered as the Long Friday — life country almost came to a halt. It drove in the importance of women’s unseen work recognizing the effect of internalizing patriarchal outlook.

While a worldwide strike of such magnitude is not feasible, we are now more aware and woke than in 1975. The first step would be recognizing the massive discrimination based on gender that is still in play in plain view. We need to acknowledge the internalized gender stereotypes. Only then can we realize the massive socio-economic cost of gender inequality and patriarchy.
